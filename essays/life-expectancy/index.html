Life Expectancy Isn’t What You’d Expect

How long will a person in a given population live, on average?

I would guess that this is the question which most people would assume ‘life expectancy’ attempts to answer. However, if you stop to think for a moment, it is not clear how we could possibly have a definite answer. After all, its all very well to retrospectively calculate the mean lifespan of historical populations who have all already died, but how can we know how long people alive today will live? We’d need to be able to predict the future, or have census bureaus hire Shinigami. It is therefore impossible to know a population’s true life expectancy (i.e. mean lifespan) in advance. There is of course actuarial life expectancy, which are insurance companies best guesses—but they are only guesses. Yet, if you Google the life expectancy for a given country, there always is a number, and it is prepared by national government’s, not insurance companies. So what does this number mean, and where does it come from?

One might guess that what is referred to as ‘life expectancy’ is actually the mean age of death. This is indeed very simple to calculate. You just look at all the people who died in a given year, and take the mean of their ages. For a population in a steady state—neither growing nor shrinking—and where death rates do not change, this would eventually match the true retrospective mean lifespan. But real populations do not exist in a steady state. If, for example, you’d recently had a population boom, with the young far outnumbering the old, this will drag down mean age of death because there will be a higher relative number of young people in the death statistics even if the young continue to die at the same lower rate as before the boom. To correct for this, demographer’s devised an ingenious solution. Instead of just looking at all deaths for a given year together, we break them down by age first and calculate the rate of death for each age. For example, we might find that in our sample 0.1% of 20 year olds died, 2% of 40 year olds died, 5% of 60 year olds died, and 10% of 80 year olds died (we’d do the same for all other ages as well). This is called a life table. Using the life table, we then use simple multiplication to determine what the distribution of lifespans (which can be represented with a graph called a survival curve) would be for a cohort born today if the life table’s exact age-specific death rates held true in perpetuity. In other words, what would the average age of death be for a person born today if their probability of death at age n in n years time is exactly the same as the probability of death for someone who is age n today. That is ‘life expectancy’.

There is a glaring problem with this. The death rates from a life table aren’t just age specific, they are cohort specific and year specific. In wartime, the death rate for young people will be high in a way it won’t be in the future, incorrectly lowering life expectancy at birth. Furthermore, if medical technology gets better then the death rates of today’s babies when they reach their 80s will be significantly lower than today’s 80 year olds. Conversely, the opposite could be true; maybe today’s baby cohort will be less healthy than their parents.
