Fly Me To The Moon

Below is an interactive simulation which allows you to pilot a rocket ship to the Moon.

This simulation is, of course, greatly simplified, in many respects–for example:

  • It is purely 2-dimensional.
  • It is wildly not-to-scale (the moon is actually much further away and much smaller).
  • The rocket itself has infinite fuel.
  • The Earth and Moon are both completely stationary.

Nevertheless, I promise that it is physically realistic in the key respects that make it so the basic principles of piloting this simulated rocketship to the moon are identical to piloting a real one. These simplifications are so that those basic principles will be all that you need to successfully land on the moon.

So, give it a go. Press up to fire the rocket and use the left and right arrow keys to set your heading (the direction the nose of the rocketship is pointing).

Falling in Style

I'll admit, flying to the moon is maybe a slightly ambitious goal to begin with. Lets start with a more immediate problem: not crashing after launch.

Getting up into the sky is easy, but to paraphrase Sir Isaac Newton, 'what goes up must come down'. You might think we just need to get far enough away from the earth that we no longer feel its gravity. However, although that is possible*, its not going to work for getting to the Moon, because the Earth's gravity is still strong there—that's why the Moon orbits the Earth, after all.

To make this clearer, let's make gravity itself visible. In this visualisation, Earth gravity is blue and Moon gravity is yellow. Where they overlap, we see a mixed green colour. The brighter the color, the stronger the pull--the weaker, the darker. The black area shows where the Earth's and Moon's gravity, pulling in opposite directions with equal force, exactly cancel each other out!

For now though, we'll get rid of the moon to keep things nice and simple. Don't worry, we'll bring it back later

Let's perform an experiment. If we drop a ball off of this tower, and launch another ball sideways from the tower with a slingshot, how long will it take each of them to land? Have a guess, and pull back on the slingshot to launch.

They hit the ground at the same time. This is because they fall at the same rate, and nothing has been done to slow the fall of either. We've neglected air resistance, of course, but this would slow the fall of both by the same amount.

However, what happens when the Earth's surface starts to curve? Try launching again with this upgraded slingshot.

It stayed airborne for longer! Let's try an even faster launch.

We can see that gravity is still curving the trajectory, but the rate of altitude loss is decreasing as we move faster, because we are getting to take advantage of more and more curvature per second whilst being subject to the same amount of gravity per second. The ground is curving away beneath the ball at closer and closer rate to that which the ball is falling.

Let's keep upgrading the slingshot and see if we can get curvature of our freefall trajectory to match the curvature of the Earth.

Ta-da! We've just achieved orbit. Specifically, a circular orbit. But what happens if we add MORE POWER?

Another orbit! This time, though, we actually gained altitude—so much altitude we went to space, in fact—resulting in this egg shaped trajectory. Note that the principle of 'What goes up must come down' is still true! After it orbits halfway around the Earth to its high point, the ball returns to its initial altitude at the tower before repeating its elliptical circuit. That highest altitude point in an orbit furthest from the Earth is called apoapsis, and the lowest altitude point of closest approach is called periapsis. When talking about Earth orbits, we use the special terms apogee and perigee for these points

If we were to launch the ball faster still, we would raise the apogee further.

Of course, the only thing separating our beautiful never-ending orbits from our previous short-lived trajectories is that they eventually hit the ground. What would happen if the balls could pass through the ground like ghosts?

We see that the dropped ball doesn't stop upon reaching the centre of the earth, but continues until it reaches its initial altitude but on the other side of the Earth—what goes down must come up, it would seem. This forms a a 1-dimensional but still endlessly repeating 'orbit'. Our previous collision trajectories, are eccentric two dimensional orbits, which, like the dropped ball, have two apogees both at the initial altitude. As they gain more launch velocity the less eccentric their orbits become and their 'perigees' inside the Earth rise closer and closer to the surface. Eventually, given the exact right amount of momentum, the orbit becomes a perfect circle with constant altitude where apogee and perigee lose all meaning. Adding more would make it elliptical again but on a perpenduclar axis.

Therefore, we do not so much as need to "achieve orbit" as achieve *an* orbit which *doesn't intersect the surface of the planet*.

Step 1 of getting to the Moon is reaching orbit around Earth. In the real-world, a stable orbit can only be achieved in space, since within the atmosphere air resistance slows us down causing us to lose altitude and eventually crash. In our simplified simulation, however, there is no atmosphere so we don't need to worry about that. Nevertheless, since we don't have a tower or a slingshot for our rocket ship, we will still want to boost upwards until we have reached an altitude where we can safely point the rocketship 'forward' in the direction we'd like to orbit—this direction is called 'prograde'.

Then, we can perform a prograde burn just like how we launched the balls. We need to keep firing prograde until our collision trajectory becomes a proper orbit. To help you out, we're going to add a projection of your trajectory so you can see how it changes live.

Congratulations, your rocketship has achieved orbit! Remember, you are still in freefall—the only reason you aren't crashing is because of your rocketship is moving so fast towards the horizon— there is therefore no way to stay still in space, you must always be in an orbit (well there is one way, but we'll get to that). From now on, then, we're not so much moving the rocketship directly as we are changing our orbit. Spoiler alert, but we need to move to a higher altitude orbit if we're going to reach the moon. Have a go at trying to achieve such an orbit. What direction should we fire the rocket?

It is intuitive to fire 'up', since we want to gain altitude. But remember, we didn't achieve orbit in the first place by firing up, because what goes up must come down. Indeed, you'll see that you gain altitude initially from this manouvre, but you fall right back down.

The issue is, as you can see, whilst this has increased the peak altitude or apoapsis, one half orbit ahead of where we fired, it hasn't lifted the whole orbit. The minimum altitude, or peripasis, remains the same. If firing raises the altitude a half orbit ahead of where we are, then if we want to raise the periapsis to 'circularise' the orbit, we must do another prograde burn at the apoapsis. Bingo! That's called a 'retro-grade' burn. One more a half orbit ahead and you'll circularise the orbit at the new lower altitude. Now, you have the power to orbit at any altitude you like! Lets test your skills.

Impulse Control

So far, you've been controlling the rocket by turning it on and off again. There hasn't been any way to control the throttle, so getting the right amount of force is a matter of timing the burn. This is actually realistic for how most rockets work! Only some special rockets have the ability to change the exhaust velocity. That's okay for normal missions though, because the amount of time spent burning is very, very small compared to the the rocketship effectively remains more or less in the same place for the duration of the burn.

Ok, clearly you've got the hang of orbital manouvreing. Its time to bring back the Moon. Before we can land on the moon, we first need to get into orbit around it. This can be achieved with what you already know, we just need to put our apoapsis in the close vicinity of the moon, so that when we reach it we are 'captured' by its gravity. Have a go.

Incredible! You're in lunar orbit. Now all we need to do is land. This, admittedly, is a bit more complex than just lowering your orbit. You essentially have to *launch* in reverse. That means firing retrograde until you have a collision trajectory, then firing 'up' in short bursts in order to slow your fall so that your landing velocity is almost zero.
